Solo Guide

  • First and foremost, don't play solo because you feel that you must. If you just want a story, read a book or watch a movie. If you just want interactivity, play a video game or a game book. You should play solo because you want to interact with a world and characters that are not available anywhere else, or in a manner not possible anywhere else.

  • Be aware that managing and providing information as a GM is a completely different experience than acquiring and acting upon information as a player, and that you're the GM in addition to being the player in solo. It's easy to get by without really understanding how the world works when someone else is telling the story, but that won't fly when you're the storyteller. Keep this in mind when selecting a setting to play in. Providing answers to questions about a preestablished setting that is outside your area of knowledge or expertise—and often even when it's within—is so much more difficult than just making it up.

  • Poke at the AP by writing only a couple notes or a paragraph at a time.

  • Write in third-person past-tense only. This lets you naturally switch the viewpoint between different characters while also letting you control how much of the character's thoughts are revealed in the prose. It may not be as immersive as first- or second-person, but it also doesn't require you to narrate in the character's voice like first-person, or feel awkward to both read and write like second-person.

  • Remember that the point of solo is to play a game and explore a world, not to tell a story. Just focus on making the current scene fun and don't worry about what happens after. You're not a storyteller, you're a worldbuilder, creating the world at the ground level, line-by-line. You're just chronicling the events as they happen, and any story emerging from that is merely incidental.

  • Even though you're not trying to craft a story, it's still important to track the tension of the scene. Interest rides with tension, and if the tension drops because there's isn't conflict, or the dialogue or narration gets lost in the weeds, the scene will lose its way forward, become less interesting, and obscure the conditions for ending. Keeping the character's goal and the various things blocking it at the forefront of the scene will layout a clear and exciting path toward its conclusion.

  • Keep descriptions and dialogue as economical as a CYOA book. Remember that you're chronicling events, not writing a novel. Contrary to standard conventions for novelized prose, it may also suit our purposes better to group sentences into paragraphs by topics, which means that multiple characters may speak or act within the same paragraph, as long as it's regarding the same topic or action.

  • Don't worry about the quality of the writing. What matters most is what you say, not how you say it. You can always wordsmith it better later. In fact, play flows better when you just summarize everything, including all dialogue, while you're in the midst of playing, and then expand it with direct speech, detailed descriptions, and introspection after. This also helps prevent meandering dialogue and exposition, which is a pitfall of writing in full detail on the first attempt.

  • Make a roll of some kind every few paragraphs to keep the story random and unexpected. Write the rolls into the AP so that the story is broken up into small blocks of text, like a CYOA section. This three-paragraph rule works best in conjunction with the first-pass summary described above, so that it expands to maybe five to seven paragraphs between rolls after.

  • Text blocks are generally structured as: one paragraph that establishes the scene and describes the result of the previous roll, then possibly one or two supplemental paragraphs that may include exposition, and finally one paragraph describing a character's next attempted action, followed by a skill check or oracle roll for that action. Rolls that don't represent actions, such as oracle questions regarding the world setting, can be performed at any time and don't alter this structure.

  • While a common bit of advice is to start the first scene of an adventure in media res, that really just means you should skip the boring, slow-burn stuff and get to the good bits right away. Rather than slowly introducing the PC to the world, start after they've already become somewhat familiar with their environment, which avoids burnout from waiting to get to what you actually want to play. One useful approach to this is to quickly summarize the story leading up to where you want to start playing, which gives a ton of solid worldbuilding for context rather than jumping in blind. You can always explain the setting and backstory with an exposition drop also.

  • It's fine if your story doesn't have much combat, but the character should still be constantly challenged physically, mentally, or socially. Every scene should have something that makes it difficult for the character to get what they want, which tests their abilities as they attempt to overcome the obstacle. Not only does this make the scene more exciting, but it places the burden on the character—whether the story takes a turn for the better or the worse, the character is the cause, not the oracle.

  • It's best to go with whatever result an oracle roll gives you on the first attempt, even if it means stepping away from the game to figure it out, because it's more fun and rewarding to accept and adapt to any and all random results that are thrown at you. This can be made easier while still playing "fair" by rolling on two tables at the same time, a generic one and a more specific one, and picking the result that works best for you.

  • For oracles with verb+noun or noun+noun pairs, sometimes interpretation is easier when you add the connector word of, as suggested by Mythic. Other helpful connectors are for, from, with, to, and against. For adjective+adjective pairs, you can add -ly to the first word or connect them with and or but. Remember, most oracles are meant to provide inspiration and spark ideas, not give explicit answers, so it's okay if your interpretation is only loosely based on the result.

  • Never ask the oracle whether your interpretation of a roll is correct, be it a yes/no oracle, a word table, a table of plot twists, or whatever. Your interpretation is always right, without question. You may ask questions for further clarification, but don't let the oracle veto your interpretation. That way leads to stalled games. If you're feeling unsure about an interpretation, brainstorm a few and then list the reasons why each one works or doesn't work with the roll and the context, then pick the one that works best with both.

  • Similar to the last, you may want to rethink using the oracle to confirm whether a cool idea or desired element is present in your story. Asking the question becomes something like "story with cool and interesting stuff on Yes, story without cool and interesting stuff on No." Don't do that to yourself. If you have a cool idea that doesn't break your story, throw it in. You can use the oracle to expand or alter your idea, if you really must roll, but don't negate it altogether.

  • If you find that your adventure has stalled, it may be that took you the "wrong path," and followed a line of thought that lead you to an uninteresting or unwanted outcome. If it's uninteresting, make it interesting by either creating a random event, or injecting something you want to see happen. If it's unwanted, then either rewind back to when you were enjoying it, or retcon it into something you want.

  • A solo adventure is comprised of three distinct pieces, even when the setting is homebrewed:

    • The campaign. This is what you'd typically find in a pre-made adventure, such as the scenario concept, character details and backstory (including daily routine and relationships), lore and history of the world, and world design (such as factions, laws, magic systems, and geography). Essentially all information which originated before the adventure began falls under the campaign. Don't ask oracle questions for the campaign, since leaving it to chance has too high a risk of generating an adventure that you won't find enjoyable. You may use tables to spark ideas, but don't treat the results as gospel.
    • The GM role. This oversees character actions and the current state of the world. Do ask oracle questions for the GM when there are multiple sensible options, since this is where the randomness comes from outside of skill checks.
    • The player role. This relates to the PC and any other characters you choose to take full control of. Don't ask questions for the player role, unless you feel like injecting randomness.
    • As an example: Don't ask whether a town has a tavern (campaign role). Don't ask whether the PC frequents the tavern often (campaign role). Don't ask whether the PC decides to have a drink at the tavern, unless you feel like it (player role). Do ask whether any other characters join them, unless it doesn't make sense that they wouldn't (GM role). Do ask whether the tavern is still open or has closed down for the night (GM role).

  • It can be really helpful to create simple tables for answering commonly asked questions. For example, a table with a number of themes that you would like to have appear in your setting, which you can roll on whenever you need more context for a random event. Another could be used to guide defining new characters for your specific setting, if your chosen system doesn't already.

  • Mythic GME is not designed for you to roll on the Characters List unless the Event Focus table indicates an NPC (and maybe Remote Event) result during a random event. For using Mythic to select a character outside of a random event, you can do any of the following:

    • Ask cascading questions on the Fate Chart. (Is it character A? No. Is it character B? No, it's character C.)
    • Choose the character that best matches a roll on the Descriptions table. ("Nicely Comforting" could mean a parental figure, a loved one, or even a pet.)
    • Create a separate Characters List for this specific location or group. (1-2: Bob, 3-4: Jane, 5-6: Tom, etc)

  • Random events in Mythic GME must always roll on the Actions meaning table to describe the event, even the New NPC result. Additional clarifying questions may be rolled on any table you want.

  • When using chaos factor in Mythic GME, questions for the oracle should be formed such that a yes adds a new element, information, or action to the scene, be it positive or negative (ie. exists/happens = yes, doesn't exist/happen = no). When adjusting the chaos factor, increase it when the scene made the adventure more complicated for your hero, even if you were successful in your scene goal, and decrease it when things got less complicated or stayed the same.

  • Write whatever the fuck you want, no matter how cringe. The only measurement of its value should be whether you find it enjoyable and entertaining, not others.

  • Use whichever oracle, system, and optional rules give the least amount of unwanted stress when playing.

  • Beyond their job or profession, every character must have a unique feature, personality, or character trait that makes them stand out as an individual compared to everyone else on the roster.

  • Though the point isn't to tell a story, the character should still have a purpose, not wander aimlessly.

    • A character should have a direction, what they seek in life, that can be categorized as adventure, security, recognition, response, or power. To represent this, the character should have something specific, tangible or not, that they desire to attain or maintain.
    • That direction, especially that thing they care about, should become threatened somehow, forcing the character to devise a general goal to remove or avoid the threat. For added effect, the most exciting goals involve the impossible, the unattainable, the forbidden, and the disastrous.
    • The character must have drive, meaning that they must truly care and be committed to attaining that goal, and they must have real reasons, whether internal or external, as to why they cannot stop. Combined with direction and goal, this becomes "desire plus danger."
    • Any worthwhile general goal can't be solved in one fell swoop, and thus is broken into many immediate goals, sometimes with multiple active act once. Each attained immediate goal causes a change in the situation, requiring one or more new immediate goal to be set, until the general goal is finally attained.

  • Likewise, each scene should have a reason to exist and contain a few required components in order to feel right.

    • A scene should begin with orientation that informs the reader of who the point-of-view character of the scene is, even if they're not the main character, and where they are.
    • Immediately thereafter the scene should describe the character's reason for being there, a goal they intend to achieve in this scene, which comes in the form of possession (something to gain or achieve), relief (something to unburden, escape, or resist), or revenge (payback for a slight, loss, or betrayal). The goal should be specific and actionable enough that it would be possible to capture the moment of success as a photo.
    • Once the orientation and goal of the scene are established, the character should face conflict, which is opposition (a character, the environment, or their own emotions) that makes the task difficult, since getting what they want for free is boring. This should be introduced either before or shortly after the character attempts to achieve their goal.
    • The character's goal in the scene is fluid and may change to counter the conflict. The scene ends when the character no longer has a goal that they can achieve right now.
    • Before wrapping up the scene, a disaster should be introduced, which is a focus on an unsolved problem (new or existing) as a hook that leads into next scene and keeps the story moving.
